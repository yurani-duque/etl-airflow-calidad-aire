1. Which capitals have the worst air?

Question. Which cities exceed the WHO guideline and how often?

Finding. 85 of 260 capitals exceed the WHO daily guideline (15 µg/m³) on more than half of their days. Riyadh, Jakarta, Santiago, Beijing, New Delhi lead the ranking, with averages between 48 and 136 µg/m³.

2. Is air quality related to emissions and income?

Question. Do countries with higher CO₂ or income have cleaner or dirtier capitals?

Finding. Across 159 countries, the rank correlation between a capital's PM2.5 and its country's CO₂ per capita is -0.10 and with GDP per capita -0.22. Urban air pollution is almost unrelated to national emissions per person and only weakly associated with lower income: local sources, geography and dust matter more than national averages. This is a correlation between countries, not causation.

3. How do capitals compare with their country?

Question. Is the capital's air worse than the national average?

Finding. 163 of 252 capitals have an average PM2.5 above their country's annual average (65 %). Capitals concentrate more pollution than the rest of the country, which supports prioritising urban air-quality policy (SDG 11.6). The country figure comes from the scraped web source.

4. How does PM2.5 evolve over time?

Question. Is there seasonality or a trend?

Finding. The highest monthly average (35.6 µg/m³) occurred in January 2025; the series stays above the 15 µg/m³ guideline in most months, so exceedance is persistent rather than occasional. Seasonal patterns motivate the month and the 7-day history used as model features.

Method and limitations

All figures query dw.fact_air_quality and its dimensions, loaded only after the Great Expectations checks passed. Country-level comparisons use the latest available value of each indicator. CSV rows are point readings and API rows are daily means; the source column keeps them apart. Correlations are across countries and do not imply causation. The forecast uses information up to day t to predict day t+1 and is evaluated on a later, unseen period.
